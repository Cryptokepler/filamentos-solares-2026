\begin{abstract}
We segment solar filaments in full-disk GONG H$\alpha$ images (MAGFiLO, $2048\times2048$) with two YOLO11-seg
instance detectors, a U-Net refiner that redraws each filament at full resolution, and a two-of-two jury.
On a fixed local exam of 121 training images the model reaches a Panoptic Quality (PQ) of 0.461
(Kaggle public 0.39), against 0.340 when one expert is scored against another. Its matched masks overlap the
expert's with a mean IoU of 0.68, above the 0.64 two experts reach with each other. We document 20 measured
ideas, most of them negative, and show that the remaining error is concentrated in small, faint filaments
on which the experts themselves disagree.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}
% [JV] Why I joined, in my own words.
Filaments are dense, cool plasma suspended above polarity inversion lines; when they erupt they can become
coronal mass ejections, and those drive geomagnetic storms. The 1859 Carrington event and the March 1989
blackout of the Qu\'ebec power grid are reminders of what such storms do to technology on Earth. Detecting
filaments automatically, every few minutes and on the whole disk, is a small but concrete step towards
watching them before they erupt.

\section{Data and evaluation}
The training set has 707 images, 296 of them annotated by two or three experts; the test set has 180. The
metric is Panoptic Quality per filament: a prediction matches an annotation when IoU $>0.5$, and
$\mathrm{PQ} = \mathrm{SQ}\cdot\frac{TP}{TP+\frac12 FP+\frac12 FN}$, with SQ the mean IoU of the matches.

Because the public leaderboard was unreliable during the competition (empty masks and leaked annotations
scored above 0.55), we never tuned on it. We fixed a local exam of 121 images ($\mathrm{md5}(\text{name})
\bmod 100 < 15$), trained only on the other 586, and scored each expert separately, pooling the counts as the
metric does. The exam noise is about $\pm0.015$ (half-split differences). Across rounds the exam predicted
the direction of the Kaggle score, with an offset of about $-0.07$.

\textbf{The human reference.} Scoring one expert against another on the 296 multi-expert images gives
PQ $=0.340$ (SQ $=0.64$). Agreement collapses for small filaments: for annotations under 330 px another expert
draws a matching filament only 41\% of the time, and 61\% of them are drawn by a single expert.

\section{Method}
The pipeline is in \texttt{filamentos/final.py} (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\textbf{Detectors.} Two YOLO11m-seg models (Ultralytics, COCO-pretrained) at 1024 px, trained on the 586
images with each expert's annotation as a separate example (2.7 h and 2.9 h on one T4). The first sees the
plain image. The second sees three views, one per channel: the original, local contrast (CLAHE) and a
black-hat map that keeps only what is darker than its surroundings and thin, removing the limb darkening.
% [JV] Where the "three views / remove the pigment" idea came from.
Each proposes everything with confidence $\ge0.1$.

\textbf{Refiner.} Detector masks at 1024 px are too coarse at the edges. A U-Net (ResNet-18 encoder) redraws
each proposal on a square crop of $1.8\times$ its size resized to 256 px, averaging four flips, at threshold
0.5. We keep only the connected component that touches the detector mask, so a neighbour inside the crop is
not swallowed. The refiner consistently adds $+0.05$ PQ (SQ $0.64\rightarrow0.68$).

\textbf{Cleaning and jury.} Per detector we drop proposals whose mean radius exceeds $0.95\,R_\odot$ (limb
artefacts) and near-duplicates (IoU $>0.5$). Proposals of the two detectors that overlap with IoU $>0.5$
form a case, which is kept only if both see it with confidence $\ge0.2$; its mask is the most confident
one. Finally, touching masks are merged, so no pixel belongs to two filaments. Inference uses only the
test image and runs on a CPU in about 6 s per image.

\textbf{Why the jury works.} Two YOLOs trained the same way fail on the same cases: a jury of models trained
on 25--100\% of the data gave no gain (0.454--0.458). The three-view model fails elsewhere, so agreement
between the two is a better signal than either confidence, and the threshold can drop to 0.2 without
inflating false positives.

\textbf{Mask quality.} Matched masks reach SQ $=0.68$, while two experts reach 0.64, and our area is 1.03
times the expert's. Thin parts (barbs, tails) were measured by morphological opening: of the expert's
structures thinner than 9 px we cover 65.5\%, another expert covers 66.9\%. Our masks are smoother (thin part
7\% of the area vs.\ 14\%) and miss some tails at the ends; growing tails along dark ridges (Frangi)
recovered them but lowered PQ (0.44--0.456), so it was not kept.

\textbf{Negative results.} All measured on the same exam: test-time flips ($-0.008$); a small-filament
specialist at 2048 px (finds 88 more filaments, invents about 170); training on more images of the same kind
(learning curve 0.428 / 0.447 / 0.440 / 0.453 at 25 / 50 / 75 / 100\%); hand-written rules and a learned
judge on crops and features (0.444); a third detector on a Frangi vesselness view (0.455 in the jury);
higher refiner thresholds; and post-processing that joins fragments or removes small masks.

\section{Where the remaining error is}
We broke down the errors of the plain YOLO model (PQ 0.453) by what another expert did in the same place.
Of the potential gain, the largest parts are filaments we ``invented'' that another expert did draw (161,
worth $+0.028$), misses that only one expert drew (182, $+0.032$) and errors on images with a single
annotator (302, $+0.05$). Errors that are clearly ours (misses confirmed by a second expert, pure inventions,
near-misses) add up to about 280, at most $+0.05$. 85\% of the misses are smaller than 3{,}000 px, exactly
where experts agree least; at 1024 px these filaments are still about 6 px wide, so resolution is not the
limit. Predicting the union of all experts would score 0.658 and copying a single expert 0.605: a model
trained on all experts learns their average judgement, and the metric rewards matching one of them.

\section{Reproducibility}
The repository contains the code, the three weight files (147 MB), \texttt{requirements.txt} with pinned
versions and \texttt{notebooks/demo\_final\_model.ipynb}, which loads the weights and writes the submission.
Every experiment has its expected result written before running and its measured result after, in
\texttt{docs/experimentos.md}.

\section{Conclusion}
A plain-image detector and a three-view detector that must agree, followed by a full-resolution refiner,
segment filaments better than one expert matches another. Further gains on this metric depend less on the
model than on how the annotations treat small, faint filaments.
